A handbook for community builders

Making Room in FOSS

A step-by-step guide to finding who your community reaches, who it misses, and what to do about it.

Working draft This is a practical self-review tool. Its five areas and scoring scale are proposed organising tools, not a validated assessment or certification of inclusion.
About 2 hours for a first full run CC BY-SA 4.0 Jump to templates

Part 1Before you start

#Who this handbook is for

Anyone who runs a FOSS community, programme or event. A college club, a city meetup, a hackathon, a project with contributors, or a national conference.

A scholarship can help someone afford an event. Work on gender representation can change who gets to speak or lead. Also consider who hears about an opportunity, what they need to take part, and what happens after their first visit.

Gender, caste, disability, language, income, location and educational background can affect participation. So can work schedules and caring responsibilities. These barriers often overlap. A free ticket may be useful to someone who still cannot afford the journey or take time off work.

Review one programme at a time. Look at what you know, hear from participants and choose actions your team can carry out. A club with two volunteers will have different options from an organisation with paid staff.

Time needed

About two hours for the first full run. Steps 1 to 5 take about 45 minutes with your team.

#Principles

  1. Programmes, not events. Who joins a community is decided long before an event. It depends on where you announce, what you assume people know, and who already leads.
  2. Reach is not inclusion. Counting attendees tells you who came. It does not tell you who stayed, who led, or who never heard about you.
  3. Measure what you act on. Many communities act without tracking results. Others track numbers they never act on. Do both, for the same thing.
  4. Collect the minimum data, with consent. Ask only what you will use. Make it optional. Report it in aggregate.
  5. Don't make under-represented people do the work. Inviting someone to join should not mean asking them to fix your community for free.
  6. Avoid tokenism. One woman on a panel or one student from a small town on a team is not representation. Support matters more than headcount.
  7. Small and finished beats large and abandoned. Two actions done well will change more than ten started and dropped.

#The five areas

AreaThe question
1. Regional accessWhere do people take part from? Which places and networks do we miss?
2. Financial barriersWhat does participation cost, and when does someone have to pay?
3. Equal opportunityWhat knowledge, language, equipment or access does the programme assume?
4. RepresentationWho participates, speaks and leads? Where does that change?
5. New voicesHow do newcomers find a role? What helps them return or contribute again?

#Step 1. Understand your community

Start with the time, money and people available to you. A project with 10,000 users and two volunteer maintainers may have less capacity than a much smaller group with paid organisers.

Use these descriptions to choose a realistic starting point. Participant numbers alone do not determine your capacity.

SmallMediumLarge
ExamplesCollege FOSS club, local meetup, small projectCity chapter, regional event, multi-college network, mid-sized projectNational conference, large project, organisation running several programmes
Active peopleUnder 5050 to 500Over 500
Organisers1 to 5 volunteersA small core team, some paid or sponsored timePaid staff or large volunteer teams
BudgetLittle or noneSome sponsorship or grantsA dedicated budget
RhythmMeets monthly or lessRegular events or releasesSeveral programmes running through the year
DecisionsInformal, by a few peopleSome written processFormal governance

You may fit more than one description. Read the suggestions for both and choose what your team can sustain.

Answer these before moving on:

  • How many people actively take part in a year?
  • How many people organise, and how many hours do they have?
  • What budget, if any, can you redirect?
  • Who makes decisions, and how?
  • What happens when the current organisers leave? (For student clubs, this is every year.)

Write the answers in Template A: Community profile.

#Step 2. Pick one programme

Don't audit the whole community at once. You will end up with a long list and no owner for any of it.

A programme is anything you run repeatedly with its own organisers: a meetup series, a hackathon, a mentoring scheme, a conference, a contributor onboarding process, a newsletter.

Choose the programme that:

  • runs again within the next six months, so you can act on what you find,
  • has an organiser who will join the audit, and
  • you suspect it reaches a narrow group.

If you are small, you may only have one programme. Start there.

#Step 3. Gather what you already know

Use the data you have before you collect anything new. Most communities hold more than they think.

Look for:

  • registration or sign-up forms,
  • attendance lists,
  • CFP submissions and selected talks,
  • contributor logs, commit history, issue trackers,
  • feedback forms,
  • scholarship or grant applications,
  • the list of current organisers and leads.

For each, note what it tells you about the five areas. Most will tell you something about regional access and representation. Few will tell you anything about financial barriers or new voices.

If you have no data for an area, write that down. That is a finding, not a failure.

#Step 4. Review and score each area

Discuss the programme with organisers and participants, including someone new or outside the team where possible. Offer a private way to give feedback, so nobody has to share a personal experience in front of the group.

Use the questions below, then choose a score that describes how you currently work.

The scale

ScoreMeaning
0We have not yet addressed this area.
1We have taken action, but have not checked whether it helped.
2We have taken action and checked the results using records, feedback or both.
3We have used what we learned to make a decision about the programme.
4We cannot describe the current position with confidence. We need to check.

The scores describe your review process. A 3 does not mean a barrier has been removed. Record participants' experiences and remaining barriers too. Do not total the scores or use them to rank communities.

Questions for each area

Regional access

  • Where do our participants come from?
  • Where do we announce, and who does that miss?
  • Could someone take part without travelling?

Financial barriers

  • What does it cost someone to take part, in total? Include travel, stay, food, devices, data and time away from work or class.
  • What support do we offer, and do people know about it before they decide?

Equal opportunity

  • What does this programme assume people already know or own?
  • Who could contribute but has no role here?
  • Is anything only available in English?

Representation

  • Who applies, who is selected, who speaks, who leads?
  • Where does representation drop?
  • Who makes decisions about this programme?

New voices

  • How many people are here for the first time?
  • How many first-timers come back?
  • When did we last hear from someone outside our usual circle?

Record scores in Template B: Scoring sheet.

#Step 5. Read your results

Read the notes and feedback first. An inaccessible venue or an account of exclusion may need attention even when the team has a good review process.

Mostly 0sChoose one barrier you can address before the next activity.
Mostly 1sCheck one existing action. Find out whether people used it and whether it helped.
One area much lower than the restInvestigate it, then compare its urgency with the other barriers raised.
Mostly 2s and 3sCheck which barriers remain. Ask participants what has changed for them.
Not enough informationDecide what you need to learn and how to do so without unnecessary data collection.
People disagreeLook at the different experiences behind their answers. They may point to a gap the organisers have missed.
Example: reviewing travel support

Suppose an event offers reimbursement but has not asked recipients whether paying upfront was difficult. That would be a 1 for financial barriers. Asking recipients and reviewing take-up could bring it to 2. If the team then offers direct booking because the feedback shows a need, it could reach 3. It would still need to check whether the budget, eligibility rules and booking process leave people out.

This is an illustration of the scale, not a report of a tested programme.

How many actions to pick

  • Small: one action. Two at most.
  • Medium: one high impact, low effort action and one high impact, high effort action.
  • Large: one of each per programme, with a named owner and budget.

Part 3 lists more actions for each area, sorted by impact and effort.

#Step 7. Plan and assign

An action without an owner will not happen.

For each action, write down:

  • what will be done,
  • who owns it,
  • what it needs: time, money, people,
  • how you will know it worked, and
  • when you will check.

Use Template C: Action plan.

If you are a student club, write the plan into your handover notes. Most club initiatives end when the organisers graduate.

#Step 8. Review and repeat

Run the audit again on the same programme. Compare your scores.

SizeWhen to review
SmallOnce a year, before handover to new organisers
MediumTwice a year
LargeAfter every edition, plus once a year across all programmes

Record changes in the scores if useful, but explain the results too. Keep reviewing unresolved barriers even when scores rise. Start on another programme when you have capacity; you do not need a particular score first.

Part 3The five areas in depth

Each area lists actions by impact and effort, signals to track, and common traps.

  • Start hereHigh impact, low effort
  • Commit yearlyHigh impact, high effort: commit to at least one each year.
  • AvoidLow-impact traps: common, feel productive, and change little.

#1. Regional access

Is this concentrated in a few cities or regions? Who can't reach it?

In India, opportunity in tech clusters around a few metros. A student in Bhagalpur or Kannur may have the skills but not the network, the information, or the train fare.

Start hereHigh impact, low effort

  • Send calls through regional colleges, local user groups and regional-language channels, not only social media.
  • Offer at least one way to take part remotely.
  • Record where participants come from, not only where the event is held.

Commit yearlyHigh impact, high effort

  • Hold events or host locations outside the usual metros, and rotate them.
  • Pair established chapters with groups in nearby smaller cities for mentoring and shared resources.

Signals to track

  • Home city or district of applicants, participants and speakers.
  • Share of participants from outside the host city.

AvoidLow-impact trap

Treating follower counts and post impressions as reach.

#2. Financial barriers

What does it cost to take part? Can we reduce it?

The cost is more than a ticket. It includes travel, a place to stay, food, a laptop, data, and days away from paid work or classes.

Start hereHigh impact, low effort

  • State the support you offer in the call itself. Don't wait for people to ask.
  • Book travel or pay upfront instead of reimbursing weeks later. Reimbursement assumes people have money to spare.
  • Offer free or low-cost tickets without heavy proof requirements.

Commit yearlyHigh impact, high effort

  • Scholarships that cover travel, stay and food, not only the ticket.
  • Stipends or grants for sustained work: organising, maintaining, mentoring.

Signals to track

  • Applications received against support available.
  • How many people offered support were able to take it up, and why others couldn't.

AvoidLow-impact trap

Long application forms or essays. These are selected for people who write confident English, not for need.

#3. Equal opportunity

Who might be excluded, and why? Does this assume a computer science background or code?

Open source needs writers, designers, translators, researchers, teachers and organisers. Most programmes are built only for people who write code.

Start hereHigh impact, low effort

  • Run first-timer support: an orientation, a buddy, a clear guide to taking part.
  • Name and credit non-code contributions: documentation, translation, design, event work.
  • Publish key calls in plain language, and in regional languages where your audience needs it.

Commit yearlyHigh impact, high effort

  • Design tracks and projects that need people from science, humanities, geography, law and public policy. Then invite them.
  • Define roles like "maintainer", "contributor" and "lead" to include non-code work, with equal recognition.

Signals to track

  • Share of participants from outside computer science and engineering.
  • Share of recognised contributions that are not code.

AvoidLow-impact trap

Saying "everyone is welcome" without changing the format, language or roles.

#4. Representation

Who participates, speaks and leads? Start with gender. Then look at region, discipline, language, institution type and disability.

Representation often drops at each stage. A group can be well represented among attendees and absent among speakers and leads.

Start hereHigh impact, low effort

  • Track representation at every stage: applicants, selected, participants, speakers, leads.
  • Use a structured rubric for selection, and a review panel that is not drawn from one group.
  • Set team composition norms, such as at least one woman or non-male member per team. Pair this with support so it does not become tokenism.

Commit yearlyHigh impact, high effort

  • Build a clear path from participant to lead: mentoring, shadowing, defined next steps.
  • Share decision-making. Rotate committee seats. Make governance open.

Signals to track

  • Representation at each stage, side by side. Look for where it drops.

AvoidLow-impact trap

A single "women in tech" panel once a year, with no change to who speaks on everything else.

#5. New voices

Who hasn't been heard yet? Do first-timers come back?

New voices need both a first opportunity and a reason to return.

Start hereHigh impact, low effort

  • Reserve speaking slots for first-time speakers, with a mentor to help them prepare.
  • Share stories from people outside the usual profile of a FOSS contributor.
  • Follow up with first-timers three to six months later.

Commit yearlyHigh impact, high effort

  • Partner with groups already doing this work: women in tech collectives, regional-language communities, disability rights groups, non-CS student bodies. Share credit and resources. Don't only recruit from them.
  • Track retention: who returns, contributes again, or takes on a role.

Signals to track

  • Share of first-time speakers and contributors.
  • Share of first-timers who return within a year.

AvoidLow-impact trap

Inviting people from under-represented groups only to speak about diversity.

Part 4Measuring without harm

You cannot improve what you cannot see. You also should not collect data people would not want collected.

#What to ask

Some of the most important barriers in India, such as caste and class, should not be asked about directly. Ignoring them is not the answer either. Use proxies:

  • home district or city,
  • type of institution: government or private, and its tier,
  • first in family to attend college,
  • language of schooling,
  • discipline of study or work.

For gender, offer open options and a "prefer not to say" choice.

#How to ask

  • Make every question optional.
  • Ask at registration, not at the door.
  • Say why you are asking and what you will do with the answers.
  • Keep the answers separate from names and contact details where you can.

#How to report

  • Report only in aggregate.
  • Don't publish any group smaller than five people. Small numbers can identify individuals.
  • Delete raw data you no longer need.

#How small communities can measure

You don't need a form. A small club can keep a simple tally after each session: how many came, how many were new, how many spoke. That is enough to score yourself honestly.

#Template D. Optional registration questions

Why we ask. We use these answers to understand who our community reaches and who it misses. Every question is optional. We report answers only in aggregate and never publish groups smaller than five people.

  1. Which city or district are you from?
  2. What type of institution did you last study at? (Government / Private / Other / Prefer not to say)
  3. Are you the first in your family to attend college? (Yes / No / Prefer not to say)
  4. What language were you mainly taught in at school?
  5. What is your field of study or work?
  6. How do you describe your gender? (Woman / Man / Non-binary / Prefer to self-describe / Prefer not to say)
  7. Is this your first time at one of our events? (Yes / No)

#Template E. Lines to add to your calls

Financial support

Non-code contributions

You don't need to write code to take part. We welcome documentation, design, translation, research, teaching and organising.

Sources and credits

This handbook builds on the work of others. Every source that shaped it is listed below, with what we used from each.

Primary source

NumFOCUS DISCOVER Cookbook

Diverse & Inclusive Spaces and Conferences: Overall Vision and Essential Resources. Produced by the NumFOCUS Diversity and Inclusion in Scientific Computing (DISC) Program, with support from the Moore Foundation, and maintained by its community of contributors.

Website: discover-cookbook.numfocus.org
Repository: github.com/numfocus/DISCOVER-Cookbook

What we used:

  • The impact and effort approach: sorting actions into high impact, low effort and high impact, high effort.
  • The advice to start with high impact, low effort actions and to do at least one high impact, high effort action.
  • The principle that you do not need to do everything, and that a few actions done well are better than many done poorly.
  • Topics that informed Parts 3 and 5, including grants and scholarships, participant and speaker selection, organising committees, newcomers and first-timers, tokenism, and assessment.

The DISCOVER Cookbook is written for conferences. This handbook adapts its approach to communities and programmes that run all year.
