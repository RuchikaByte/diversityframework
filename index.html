A handbook for community builders

Making Room in FOSS

A step-by-step guide to finding who your community reaches, who it misses, and what to do about it.

Working draft This is a practical self-review tool. Its five areas and scoring scale are proposed organising tools, not a validated assessment or certification of inclusion.
About 2 hours for a first full run CC BY-SA 4.0 Jump to templates

Part 1 Before you start

Who this handbook is for

It's for anyone who runs a FOSS community, programme or event. That could be a college club, a city meetup, a hackathon, a project with contributors, or a national conference.

A scholarship helps someone afford an event. Work on gender representation changes who gets to speak or lead. Both matter, but they're only part of it. It also matters who hears about an opportunity, what they need to take part, and what happens after their first visit.

Gender, caste, disability, language, income, location and educational background can affect participation. So can work schedules and caring responsibilities, and these often overlap. A free ticket doesn't help much if someone still can't afford the journey or take time off work.

This handbook takes you through reviewing one programme at a time. You'll look at what you already know, hear from participants, and choose actions your team can carry out. A club with two volunteers won't have the same options as an organisation with paid staff, and that's fine.

Time needed

The first full run takes about two hours. Steps 1 to 5 take about 45 minutes with your team.

Principles

  1. Programmes, not events. Who ends up in a community is decided long before any event. It depends on where you announce, what you assume people already know, and who's already leading.
  2. Reach is not inclusion. Counting attendees tells you who came. It doesn't tell you who stayed, who went on to lead, or who never heard about you.
  3. Measure what you act on. Some communities act without ever checking results. Others collect numbers and never act on them. Try to do both, for the same thing.
  4. Collect the minimum data, with consent. Only ask for what you'll use. Make it optional, and report it in aggregate.
  5. Don't make under-represented people do the work. Inviting someone in shouldn't mean asking them to fix your community for free.
  6. Avoid tokenism. One woman on a panel, or one student from a small town on a team, isn't representation. Supporting people matters more than counting them.
  7. Small and finished beats large and abandoned. Two actions done well will change more than ten that get started and dropped.

The five areas

AreaThe question
1. Regional accessWhere do people take part from? Which places and networks do we miss?
2. Financial barriersWhat does participation cost, and when does someone have to pay?
3. Equal opportunityWhat knowledge, language, equipment or access does the programme assume?
4. RepresentationWho participates, speaks and leads? Where does that change?
5. New voicesHow do newcomers find a role? What helps them return or contribute again?

Step 1. Understand your community

Start with the time, money and people you actually have. A project with 10,000 users and two volunteer maintainers may have less capacity than a much smaller group with paid organisers.

Use the table below to pick a realistic starting point. Don't go by participant numbers alone.

SmallMediumLarge
ExamplesCollege FOSS club, local meetup, small projectCity chapter, regional event, multi-college network, mid-sized projectNational conference, large project, organisation running several programmes
Active peopleUnder 5050 to 500Over 500
Organisers1 to 5 volunteersA small core team, some paid or sponsored timePaid staff or large volunteer teams
BudgetLittle or noneSome sponsorship or grantsA dedicated budget
RhythmMeets monthly or lessRegular events or releasesSeveral programmes running through the year
DecisionsInformal, by a few peopleSome written processFormal governance

If you fit more than one column, read the suggestions for both and pick what your team can keep up.

Answer these before moving on:

  • How many people actively take part in a year?
  • How many people organise, and how many hours do they have?
  • What budget, if any, can you redirect?
  • Who makes decisions, and how?
  • What happens when the current organisers leave? (For student clubs, this is every year.)

Write the answers in Template A: Community profile.

Step 2. Pick one programme

Don't try to audit the whole community at once. You'll end up with a long list and no one responsible for any of it.

A programme is anything you run repeatedly with its own organisers: a meetup series, a hackathon, a mentoring scheme, a conference, a contributor onboarding process, a newsletter.

Choose the programme that:

  • runs again within the next six months, so you can act on what you find,
  • has an organiser who will join the audit, and
  • you suspect it reaches a narrow group.

If you're a small group, you might only have one programme. Start with that.

Step 3. Gather what you already know

Before collecting anything new, look at what you already have. It's usually more than you'd expect.

Look for:

  • registration or sign-up forms,
  • attendance lists,
  • CFP submissions and selected talks,
  • contributor logs, commit history, issue trackers,
  • feedback forms,
  • scholarship or grant applications,
  • the list of current organisers and leads.

Go through each one and note what it tells you about the five areas. You'll probably find a fair bit about regional access and representation, and very little about financial barriers or new voices.

If you have nothing for an area, write that down. Knowing what you don't track is useful in itself.

Step 4. Review and score each area

Talk the programme through with organisers and participants. If you can, include someone new or from outside the team. Also give people a private way to share feedback, so nobody has to talk about a personal experience in front of the group.

Go through the questions below, then pick the score that best describes how you work right now.

The scale

ScoreMeaning
0We haven't worked on this area yet.
1We've taken action, but haven't checked whether it helped.
2We've taken action and checked the results, using records, feedback or both.
3We've used what we learned to make a decision about the programme.

The scores are about your review process, not about how inclusive you are. A 3 doesn't mean a barrier is gone. Write down what participants told you and which barriers are still there. Don't add up the scores or use them to compare communities.

Questions for each area

Regional access

  • Where do our participants come from?
  • Where do we announce, and who does that miss?
  • Could someone take part without travelling?

Financial barriers

  • What does it cost someone to take part, in total? Include travel, stay, food, devices, data and time away from work or class.
  • What support do we offer, and do people know about it before they decide?

Equal opportunity

  • What does this programme assume people already know or own?
  • Who could contribute but has no role here?
  • Is anything only available in English?

Representation

  • Who applies, who is selected, who speaks, who leads?
  • Where does representation drop?
  • Who makes decisions about this programme?

New voices

  • How many people are here for the first time?
  • How many first-timers come back?
  • When did we last hear from someone outside our usual circle?

Record scores in Template B: Scoring sheet.

Step 5. Read your results

Start with the notes and feedback, then look at the scores. If someone found the venue hard to access or felt left out, that needs attention even if your scores look good.

Mostly 0sChoose one barrier you can address before the next activity.
Mostly 1sCheck one existing action. Find out whether people used it and whether it helped.
One area much lower than the restLook into it, then weigh it against the other barriers people raised.
Mostly 2s and 3sCheck which barriers remain. Ask participants what has changed for them.
Not enough informationWork out what you need to find out, and how to do it without collecting more data than you need.
People disagreeTalk about why. Different experiences often point to a gap the organisers haven't noticed.
Example: reviewing travel support

Say an event reimburses travel, but has never asked people whether paying upfront was hard for them. That's a 1 for financial barriers. If the team asks recipients and looks at who took up the support, that's a 2. If they then switch to booking travel directly because of what they heard, that's a 3. Even then, they'd still need to check whether the budget, eligibility rules or booking process leave anyone out.

This is a made-up example to show how the scale works.

The tables below suggest actions based on your size. Pick the ones that match what you found, and check you have the time and money for them.

If you scored 0 or 1, start with the first column. If you scored 2 or 3, use what you learned to improve something you already do, or try something new. Ideas from either column might work.

How many actions to pick

  • Small: one action. Two at most.
  • Medium: one high impact, low effort action and one high impact, high effort action.
  • Large: one of each per programme, with a named owner and budget.

There are more ideas for each area in Part 3, sorted by impact and effort. To see how a small club might choose, look at the worked example.

Step 7. Plan and assign

If nobody owns an action, it won't happen.

For each action, write down:

  • what will be done,
  • who owns it,
  • what it needs: time, money, people,
  • how you will know it worked, and
  • when you will check.

Use Template C: Action plan.

If you're a student club, put the plan in your handover notes. Most club initiatives stop when the organisers graduate.

Step 8. Review and repeat

After a while, run the audit again on the same programme and compare.

SizeWhen to review
SmallOnce a year, before handover to new organisers
MediumTwice a year
LargeAfter every edition, plus once a year across all programmes

You can note how the scores changed, but write down what's behind them too. Keep an eye on barriers that haven't been solved, even if your scores go up. When you have the capacity, start on another programme. You don't need to reach a certain score first.

Part 3 The five areas in depth

For each area, you'll find actions sorted by impact and effort, things to track, and common traps.

  • Start here High impact, low effort
  • Commit yearly High impact, high effort: try to do at least one a year.
  • Avoid Low-impact traps: they're common and feel productive, but change very little.

1. Regional access

Is this concentrated in a few cities or regions? Who can't reach it?

In India, most tech opportunities are concentrated in a few big cities. A student in Bhagalpur or Kannur might have the skills, but not the contacts, the information or the train fare.

Start here High impact, low effort

  • Share calls through regional colleges, local user groups and regional-language channels, as well as social media.
  • Offer at least one way to take part remotely.
  • Record where participants are from, as well as where the event is held.

Commit yearly High impact, high effort

  • Hold events or host locations outside the usual metros, and rotate them.
  • Pair established chapters with groups in nearby smaller cities for mentoring and shared resources.

Signals to track

  • Home city or district of applicants, participants and speakers.
  • Share of participants from outside the host city.

Avoid Low-impact trap

Treating follower counts and post impressions as a measure of reach.

2. Financial barriers

What does it cost to take part? Can we reduce it?

Taking part costs more than a ticket. There's travel, somewhere to stay, food, a laptop, mobile data, and days away from paid work or classes.

Start here High impact, low effort

  • Mention the support you offer in the call itself, so people don't have to ask.
  • Book travel or pay upfront instead of reimbursing weeks later. Reimbursement only works for people who can afford to pay first.
  • Offer free or low-cost tickets without heavy proof requirements.

Commit yearly High impact, high effort

  • Offer scholarships that cover travel, stay and food, as well as the ticket.
  • Offer stipends or grants for ongoing work: organising, maintaining, mentoring.

Signals to track

  • Applications received against support available.
  • How many people offered support were able to take it up, and why others couldn't.

Avoid Low-impact trap

Long application forms or essays. These tend to favour people who write confident English over the people who need support most.

3. Equal opportunity

Who might be excluded, and why? Does this assume a computer science background or code?

Open source needs writers, designers, translators, researchers, teachers and organisers. But most programmes are designed only for people who write code.

Start here High impact, low effort

  • Support first-timers with an orientation, a buddy and a clear guide to taking part.
  • Name and credit non-code contributions: documentation, translation, design, event work.
  • Publish key calls in plain language, and in regional languages where your audience needs it.

Commit yearly High impact, high effort

  • Design tracks and projects that need people from science, humanities, geography, law and public policy. Then invite them.
  • Define roles like "maintainer", "contributor" and "lead" to include non-code work, with equal recognition.

Signals to track

  • Share of participants from outside computer science and engineering.
  • Share of recognised contributions that are not code.

Avoid Low-impact trap

Saying "everyone is welcome" but keeping the same format, language and roles.

4. Representation

Who participates, speaks and leads? Start with gender. Then look at region, discipline, language, institution type and disability.

Representation often drops off at each stage. A group can be well represented among attendees and missing entirely from speakers and leads.

Start here High impact, low effort

  • Track representation at every stage: applicants, selected, participants, speakers, leads.
  • Use a structured rubric for selection, and a review panel that is not drawn from one group.
  • Set team composition norms, such as at least one woman or non-male member per team. Back it up with support, so it doesn't turn into tokenism.

Commit yearly High impact, high effort

  • Build a clear path from participant to lead: mentoring, shadowing, defined next steps.
  • Share decision-making, rotate committee seats and keep governance open.

Signals to track

  • Representation at each stage, side by side, so you can see where it drops.

Avoid Low-impact trap

Holding one "women in tech" panel a year, while the same people speak on everything else.

5. New voices

Who hasn't been heard yet? Do first-timers come back?

People need a first chance to take part, and a reason to come back.

Start here High impact, low effort

  • Reserve speaking slots for first-time speakers, with a mentor to help them prepare.
  • Share stories from people outside the usual profile of a FOSS contributor.
  • Follow up with first-timers three to six months later.

Commit yearly High impact, high effort

  • Partner with groups already doing this work: women in tech collectives, regional-language communities, disability rights groups, non-CS student bodies. Share credit and resources with them, rather than just recruiting from them.
  • Track retention: who returns, contributes again, or takes on a role.

Signals to track

  • Share of first-time speakers and contributors.
  • Share of first-timers who return within a year.

Avoid Low-impact trap

Inviting people from under-represented groups to speak only about diversity.

Part 4 Measuring without harm

You need some data to know whether things are getting better. But you shouldn't collect anything people wouldn't want collected.

What to ask

Some of the biggest barriers in India, like caste and class, shouldn't be asked about directly. But you can't ignore them either. Instead, ask about things that point to them:

  • home district or city,
  • type of institution: government or private, and its tier,
  • first in family to attend college,
  • language of schooling,
  • discipline of study or work.

For gender, offer open options and a "prefer not to say" choice.

How to ask

  • Make every question optional.
  • Ask at registration, not at the door.
  • Say why you're asking and what you'll do with the answers.
  • Keep the answers separate from names and contact details where you can.

How to report

  • Report only in aggregate.
  • Don't publish numbers for any group smaller than five people. Small numbers can identify individuals.
  • Delete raw data you no longer need.

How small communities can measure

You don't need a form. A small club can keep a simple tally after each session: how many came, how many were new, how many spoke. That's enough to score yourself honestly.

Template D. Optional registration questions

Why we ask. We use these answers to understand who our community reaches and who it misses. Every question is optional. We report answers only in aggregate and never publish groups smaller than five people.

  1. Which city or district are you from?
  2. What type of institution did you last study at? (Government / Private / Other / Prefer not to say)
  3. Are you the first in your family to attend college? (Yes / No / Prefer not to say)
  4. What language were you mainly taught in at school?
  5. What is your field of study or work?
  6. How do you describe your gender? (Woman / Man / Non-binary / Prefer to self-describe / Prefer not to say)
  7. Is this your first time at one of our events? (Yes / No)

Template E. Lines to add to your calls

Financial support

We cover travel and stay for speakers, booked in advance. Attendees can apply for travel support with a short form. You don't need to explain your circumstances in detail.

Non-code contributions

You don't need to write code to take part. We welcome documentation, design, translation, research, teaching and organising.

NumFOCUS DISCOVER Cookbook

Diverse & Inclusive Spaces and Conferences: Overall Vision and Essential Resources. Produced by the NumFOCUS Diversity and Inclusion in Scientific Computing (DISC) Program, with support from the Moore Foundation, and maintained by its community of contributors.

Website: discover-cookbook.numfocus.org

What we used:

  • The impact and effort approach: sorting actions into high impact, low effort and high impact, high effort.
  • The advice to start with high impact, low effort actions and to do at least one high impact, high effort action.
  • The principle that you don't need to do everything, and that a few actions done well are better than many done poorly.

The DISCOVER Cookbook is written for conferences. This handbook adapts its approach to communities and programmes that run all year.
